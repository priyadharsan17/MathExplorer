1665 – 1684 CE

Calculus — Newton vs Leibniz

Two of history's greatest minds independently invented the same mathematics in the same decade — then spent the rest of their lives in one of science's most bitter priority disputes.

The Problem

What forced this invention?

In 1665, Isaac Newton was a 22-year-old student at Cambridge when the bubonic plague closed the university. Sent home to Woolsthorpe Manor in Lincolnshire, he had eighteen months of uninterrupted thinking time — and he used it to break physics.

Newton needed to answer a question no one had fully solved: how fast is something moving at a single instant? Average speed over a journey was easy. But the speed of a cannonball at the precise moment it left the barrel — before any time had elapsed — seemed to require dividing zero by zero. Classical geometry had no answer.

Meanwhile in Paris, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz was attacking a different but related question: how do you find the exact area under an arbitrary curved line? Approximating with rectangles worked, but the finer you made the rectangles, the more rectangles you needed — infinitely many rectangles of infinitely small width. How do you add up infinitely many infinitely small things?

Both problems — instantaneous rates and infinite sums — were two faces of the same mathematical coin. The tool to solve both of them became calculus.

The People

🍎
Isaac Newton
1643 – 1727
Natural Philosopher, Mathematician, Theologian — England

Newton invented what he called the method of fluxions during 1665–1666, the so-called "annus mirabilis" (miracle year). In eighteen months of isolation he also derived his law of universal gravitation, decomposed white light into its spectrum, and laid the foundation for classical mechanics. He did not publish his calculus until 1687, buried inside the Principia Mathematica.

🖋️
Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz
1646 – 1716
Mathematician, Philosopher, Diplomat — Germany

Leibniz independently developed calculus between 1673 and 1676, working in Paris as a diplomat. Where Newton thought geometrically, Leibniz thought symbolically — he was obsessed with finding the perfect notation. His paper of 1684 was the first published account of differential calculus, using the notation \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) and \(\int\) that every student uses today.

The Breakthrough

Both men made the same core discovery, expressed differently. The key insight was that finding the slope of a curve at a point (differentiation) and finding the area under a curve (integration) are exact inverses of one another. This connection — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus — is arguably the most important theorem in mathematics.

The Derivative (instantaneous rate of change)
\[ f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x+h) - f(x)}{h} \]
The slope of the curve \(f\) at any point \(x\). Newton's "fluxion" expressed the same idea using dot notation: \(\dot{x}\).
The Integral (area under a curve)
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{n \to \infty} \sum_{k=1}^{n} f(x_k)\,\Delta x \]
The exact area as the width \(\Delta x\) of each rectangle shrinks to zero and their count grows to infinity.
The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus
\[ \int_a^b f'(x)\,dx = f(b) - f(a) \]
Differentiation and integration are inverse operations. To integrate \(f'\) over \([a, b]\), just evaluate the original function \(f\) at the endpoints.

The Notation Battle

Newton and Leibniz expressed identical mathematics in completely different notation. The priority dispute turned political — Britain loyally used Newton's notation for a century while continental Europe used Leibniz's. British mathematics fell behind as a result. Leibniz's notation ultimately won, because it is more flexible and reveals the structure of calculations more clearly.

Why Leibniz won: His notation \(\frac{dy}{dx}\) makes the chain rule intuitive — fractions "cancel" symbolically: \(\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy}{du} \cdot \frac{du}{dx}\). Newton's dot notation has no such visual aid. Today, Newton's dot notation survives only in physics for time derivatives (e.g. \(\dot{x}\) for velocity).

Timeline

~250 BCE
Archimedes — Method of Exhaustion
Archimedes computes the area of a parabola and the volume of a sphere by exhausting them with polygons — the earliest known forerunner of integration.
1609
Kepler's Area Law
Johannes Kepler discovers that planets sweep equal areas in equal times — requiring ideas about instantaneous change that prefigure calculus.
1629–1637
Fermat's Method of Maxima
Pierre de Fermat develops a technique to find the maxima of curves by setting a "nearly zero" quantity to zero — an informal version of differentiation.
1665 – 1666
Newton's "Annus Mirabilis"
During the plague years at Woolsthorpe Manor, Newton invents the method of fluxions, the law of gravitation, and discovers the spectrum of light. His calculus notes circulate privately among a small group but are not published.
1673 – 1676
Leibniz Develops His Calculus Independently
Working in Paris, Leibniz independently discovers differential and integral calculus. He chooses his notation deliberately: \(\int\) is a long "S" for summa (sum); \(d\) stands for differentia.
1684
Leibniz Publishes First
Leibniz publishes Nova Methodus — the first published account of differential calculus. Newton has still not published his work publicly.
1687
Newton's Principia Mathematica
Newton finally publishes his Principia — the laws of motion and universal gravitation. The calculus underpinning it is buried in geometric form; he uses fluxion notation sparingly.
1699 – 1716
The Priority Dispute
The Royal Society investigates the priority question. Newton (who investigates himself) is declared the sole inventor. Continental mathematicians side with Leibniz. The feud fractures European mathematics for a generation. Leibniz dies in 1716, largely forgotten by the scientific establishment, with only his secretary at his funeral.
18th–19th Century
The Aftermath
The Bernoulli brothers, Euler, Lagrange, and Laplace develop calculus into analysis using Leibniz's notation. British mathematicians, loyal to Newton's notation, fall a generation behind. Cauchy and Weierstrass eventually provide the rigorous foundation (limits) that both Newton and Leibniz had relied on informally.
Today
The Verdict of History
The modern consensus: both invented calculus independently. Newton invented it first (~1666) but Leibniz published first (1684) and gave us the notation. Both deserve full credit.

Why It Still Matters

Modern Impact

It is almost impossible to overstate the importance of calculus. Every branch of physics, engineering, economics, and computer science is built on it. Without calculus:

  • No aerospace engineering — aircraft wings, rocket trajectories, and orbital mechanics all require differential equations.
  • No electrical engineering — AC circuits, signal processing, and antenna design are described by calculus.
  • No medical imaging — MRI and CT scans use integral transforms (Fourier analysis) to reconstruct images from raw signals.
  • No machine learning — training neural networks is an optimisation problem solved by gradient descent, which is pure calculus.
  • No financial markets — the Black-Scholes formula for option pricing is a partial differential equation.
