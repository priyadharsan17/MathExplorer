The Origin of Numbers & Counting

Before writing, before cities, before agriculture — humans were already counting. The tally stick predates every civilisation, and the digit 0–9 you type today is the final form of a 35,000-year journey.

The Problem

What forced this invention?

Counting is so natural to modern humans that it is difficult to imagine a world without numbers. Yet for most of human history, there were no numerals, no arithmetic, and no way to record a quantity except as a physical mark on a physical surface.

The earliest counting arose from practical necessity: how many animals were in a herd? How many days until the next full moon? How many fish were exchanged for grain? Without a reliable way to record and compare quantities, trade, astronomy, and agriculture were all severely limited.

The first solution was the tally — a notch on a bone or stick, one mark per item counted. Simple, but it worked. The problem came later: as quantities grew larger and trade networks more complex, pure tallying became unmanageable. You cannot quickly distinguish a row of 57 notches from 58. Grouping was the next leap: bundle five tallies, then count the bundles. From this intuition every positional number system in history ultimately grew.

But even grouped tallies are not numerals. The final problem was abstraction: can you write a symbol that means "five" regardless of what is being counted? That leap — from five apples to the abstract idea of fiveness — is what separates tallying from genuine mathematics.

The People — & The Civilisations

🦴
Unknown Homo sapiens
~43,000 – 35,000 BCE
Swaziland / Southern Africa

The Lebombo Bone — a baboon fibula with 29 deliberate notches — is the oldest known mathematical object, found in a cave in the Lebombo Mountains. Its creator is unknown. The notch count of 29 may record a lunar month, suggesting early humans were already doing astronomy.

🏛️
The Babylonians
~3,000 – 300 BCE
Mesopotamia (modern Iraq)

Scribes of ancient Mesopotamia developed a base-60 (sexagesimal) positional number system pressed into wet clay with a stylus. This system was capable of representing very large numbers and fractions. Its legacy survives in our use of 60 seconds in a minute, 60 minutes in an hour, and 360 degrees in a circle.

📜
The Egyptians
~3,000 – 1,000 BCE
Nile Valley, North Africa

Egyptian mathematicians used a base-10 system with separate hieroglyphic symbols for 1, 10, 100, 1,000, 10,000, 100,000, and 1,000,000. The Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (~1,650 BCE) shows sophisticated use of unit fractions. However, Egyptian numerals were not positional — position did not change a symbol's value.

🔢
Indian Mathematicians
~500 BCE – 700 CE
Indian Subcontinent

Indian mathematicians created the Hindu-Arabic numeral system — the digits 0–9 with positional notation — that every modern culture now uses. The Brahmi script (3rd century BCE) is the ancestor of the modern digits. Combined with Brahmagupta's formalisation of zero (628 CE), this system could represent any number with just ten symbols.

The Breakthrough — Positional Notation

The single most important advance in the history of numbers was not any individual discovery — it was the idea that a digit's position determines its value. That insight made it possible to write any number, no matter how large, with a small finite set of symbols.

Non-Positional (Egyptian / Roman)
MMXXVI = 2026
Symbols are simply added together. No symbol changes value based on where it appears. Adding large numbers requires counting symbols — there is no "carrying."
Positional (Hindu-Arabic)
\[ 2026 = 2{\times}10^3 + 0{\times}10^2 + 2{\times}10^1 + 6{\times}10^0 \]
Each digit's value depends on its column. The same digit "2" means 2,000 in the thousands column and 20 in the tens column. This is why arithmetic algorithms (long addition, long division) work.
Babylonian Base-60
\[ 1{,}30 \text{ (base 60)} = 1{\times}60 + 30 = 90 \]
The Babylonian system was positional before the Hindu-Arabic one, but used base 60 and lacked a true zero (only a placeholder). Its influence survives in our measurements of time and angles.
Why base 60? Sixty has more divisors than any smaller number: it divides evenly by 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20, 30, and 60. For a pre-calculator world where fractions were calculated by hand, a highly composite base made division and trade much simpler.

Timeline — From Tally Bones to Digits

~43,000 BCE
Lebombo Bone
A baboon fibula with 29 deliberate notches is carved in what is now Swaziland (eSwatini). It is the oldest known mathematical artefact. The 29 notches may track a lunar cycle — the earliest known attempt to count days.
~20,000 BCE
Ishango Bone
Found in the Democratic Republic of Congo, the Ishango Bone has notch groupings that some mathematicians interpret as evidence of counting by doubling and possibly prime-like groupings. Others see a simple tally. Whatever its purpose, the deliberate grouping shows abstract numerical thinking.
~3,000 BCE
Babylonian Base-60 Numerals
Mesopotamian scribes develop a fully positional base-60 number system pressed into clay tablets with a reed stylus. They can represent large fractions and perform the equivalent of multiplication tables. This is the earliest known positional number system.
~3,000 BCE
Egyptian Hieroglyphic Numerals
Egyptian scribes develop a base-10 system using distinct hieroglyphs for each power of ten. Non-positional, but capable of representing large numbers by repeating symbols. Used for land surveys, tax records, and the construction projects of the Old Kingdom.
~1,650 BCE
Rhind Mathematical Papyrus
The most complete surviving Egyptian mathematical document. Written by scribe Ahmes, it contains 84 practical problems involving fractions, areas, and volumes. Egyptians expressed all fractions as sums of unit fractions (fractions with numerator 1) — a constraint that produced some ingenious methods.
~300 BCE
Babylonian Placeholder Zero
Babylonian scribes introduce a two-wedge symbol to mark an empty column in their positional system. This is the earliest known zero symbol — but it is purely a placeholder, not a number. You could not add it, subtract from it, or define arithmetic rules for it.
~3rd century BCE
Brahmi Numerals — India
Indian mathematicians begin developing the Brahmi numeral system, the ancestor of the modern digits 1–9. Unlike Roman or Egyptian numerals, these symbols are not combinations of simpler marks — each digit is a distinct symbol. When combined with positional notation and zero, this becomes the Hindu-Arabic system.
628 CE
Brahmagupta Formalises Zero
Brahmagupta defines zero as a number — not just a placeholder — with full arithmetic rules. Combined with the existing Brahmi digits and positional notation, this completes the Hindu-Arabic numeral system: any number can be written with the ten symbols 0–9.
825 CE
Al-Khwārizmī Spreads Hindu-Arabic Numerals
Al-Khwārizmī writes On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals, carrying the Indian system westward through the Islamic world. His name gives us the word algorithm.
1202
Fibonacci Introduces Digits to Europe
Liber Abaci argues for the superiority of Hindu-Arabic numerals over Roman numerals for calculation. European merchants and scholars slowly adopt the modern digit system over the following two centuries.
Today
0–9 Everywhere
The ten Hindu-Arabic digits — refined over millennia from tally bones and clay tablets — are used by virtually every culture on Earth. They underpin every calculator, computer, and scientific instrument ever built.

The Same Number in Four Systems

The number 42 written across four historical numeral systems shows how dramatically the notation changed — while the underlying quantity stayed identical.

𒐏𒐘
Babylonian Cuneiform (~3,000 BCE)
Two cuneiform groups: four tens (𒐏) and two units (𒐘). Written on wet clay with a reed stylus. Base-60 positional.
𓎉𓏻
Egyptian Hieroglyphic (~3,000 BCE)
Four tens (arch symbols) and two units (strokes). Non-positional — symbols are simply counted and added.
XLII
Roman Numerals
XL (40) + II (2). Uses subtraction notation (XL = 50 − 10). Non-positional and difficult to use for arithmetic operations.
42
Hindu-Arabic (Modern)
Positional base-10: digit 4 in the tens column (value = 40) and digit 2 in the ones column (value = 2). The system that won.

Why It Still Matters

Modern Impact

Positional numeration is the invisible foundation of all quantitative thought:

  • Arithmetic algorithms — column addition, long multiplication, long division all work because of positional notation. They are impossible with Roman numerals.
  • Binary and computing — computers use base-2 (binary), an extreme version of positional notation with only two digits (0 and 1). Every byte, every pixel, every encrypted message is positional arithmetic.
  • Scientific notation — \(6.022 \times 10^{23}\) (Avogadro's number) is only writable because we have a positional system with exponents. Without it, astronomy and chemistry would be impractical.
  • Time and angles — our base-60 inheritance from Babylon shows up every day: 60 seconds, 60 minutes, 360 degrees, 60 arc-minutes, 60 arc-seconds.
  • Cryptography — modern encryption (RSA, elliptic curves) operates on astronomically large numbers. Representing and computing with such numbers requires positional notation.

In Their Words

The method of the Indians surpasses all other methods for its ease and quickness and requires little effort on the part of those who calculate. Its computing capacity grows to infinity.

— Al-Kindī (9th century CE), describing the Hindu numeral system

Key Takeaways

  • ✦

    The oldest known mathematical artefact is the Lebombo Bone (~43,000 BCE) — a notched baboon fibula that may record a lunar month.

  • ✦

    The critical invention was positional notation: a digit's value depends on its position. The Babylonians (base-60) invented this first; Indians perfected it in base-10 with the digits 0–9.

  • ✦

    We still use Babylonian base-60 every day: 60 seconds per minute, 60 minutes per hour, 360 degrees in a circle.

  • ✦

    The Hindu-Arabic numeral system (0–9) spread to Europe via Al-Khwārizmī (825 CE) and Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202). It replaced Roman numerals for calculation within a few centuries.

  • ✦

    Every computer in existence uses binary (base-2) — a direct descendant of the positional idea that Babylonian scribes first pressed into clay 5,000 years ago.
