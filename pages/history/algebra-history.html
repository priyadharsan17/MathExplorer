820 CE

Algebra — The Art of Solving

A 9th-century Islamic scholar's practical guide to dividing inheritances gave mathematics a new name, a systematic method — and the English language the word algorithm.

The Problem

What forced this invention?

In 9th-century Baghdad, Islamic law was precise about inheritance: a deceased man's estate must be divided among his heirs according to detailed rules in the Quran — widow receives one-eighth, daughters receive half of a son's share, and so on. The problem was purely mathematical: given an estate's value, how do you calculate each heir's exact share?

These weren't abstract puzzles. Judges and merchants needed reliable methods they could apply step by step, without expert mathematicians at hand. Worse, existing Greek mathematics — Euclid's geometry — offered theorems and proofs but no practical computational procedures. You couldn't hand a judge a copy of Elements and expect results.

What was needed was a systematic method for reducing an unknown quantity in an equation — moving terms from one side to the other, keeping both sides balanced — until the unknown stood alone. That method had no name, no dedicated book, and no single inventor. Until 820 CE.

The People

📚
Muḥammad ibn Mūsā al-Khwārizmī
~780 – ~850 CE
Mathematician, Astronomer — House of Wisdom, Baghdad

Al-Khwārizmī was a scholar at the famous House of Wisdom (Bayt al-Ḥikma) in Baghdad — the greatest intellectual centre of the medieval world. He wrote the first systematic treatise on solving equations. His name, Latinised as Algoritmi, gave us the word algorithm. The title of his book gave us the word algebra.

🏛️
Diophantus of Alexandria
~210 – ~290 CE
Mathematician — Alexandria, Egypt

Five centuries before al-Khwārizmī, Diophantus wrote Arithmetica — a collection of 130 problems involving equations. He introduced syncopated notation (abbreviations for common operations) and was the first to treat fractions as numbers. He is often called the "father of algebra" for this work, though his approach was unsystematic compared to al-Khwārizmī's.

🔢
Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci)
~1170 – ~1250 CE
Mathematician — Pisa, Italy

Fibonacci studied in North Africa where he encountered Islamic mathematics. His 1202 book Liber Abaci ("Book of Calculation") introduced al-Khwārizmī's methods and the Hindu-Arabic numeral system to Europe. Without Fibonacci, algebra — and decimal numbers — might have taken another century to reach the West.

Words We Use Every Day

Two everyday words in science and computing trace directly to one man and one book written in 820 CE.

Algebra
From Arabic: al-jabr
Title of al-Khwārizmī's book: Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābala ("The Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing"). Al-jabr means "restoration" — moving a subtracted term to the other side of an equation.
Algorithm
From Latin: Algoritmi
Medieval European scholars translated al-Khwārizmī's name as Algoritmi. Books on Hindu-Arabic arithmetic were titled "Algoritmi de numero Indorum" — "Algoritmi on the Indian numbers." The author's name became the method.

The Breakthrough

Al-Khwārizmī's great contribution was not a new theorem — it was a systematic procedure. He described two complementary operations that could reduce any linear or quadratic equation to a standard form:

Al-Jabr — "Restoration"
\[ x^2 = 40x - 4x^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 5x^2 = 40x \]
Moving a subtracted quantity from one side to the other — adding it to both sides to restore the equation's balance.
Al-Muqābala — "Balancing"
\[ 50 + x^2 = 29 + 10x \quad\Rightarrow\quad 21 + x^2 = 10x \]
Reducing like terms on both sides simultaneously — cancelling equal quantities from each side to simplify.
Geometric proofs: Al-Khwārizmī proved his quadratic solutions geometrically — completing the square was a literal operation: he drew a square, added rectangular pieces to it, then completed it into a larger square. The algebraic formula \(x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}\) came later; his was a visual, physical argument that anyone could verify with a ruler.
Completing the Square — Al-Khwārizmī's method
\[ x^2 + 10x = 39 \]
Add \(\left(\frac{10}{2}\right)^2 = 25\) to both sides: \(x^2 + 10x + 25 = 64\), so \((x+5)^2 = 64\), giving \(x = 3\). He proved this by literally drawing a square of side \(x\), attaching four rectangles of width \(\frac{10}{4}\), and completing the corners.

Timeline — From Ancient Equations to Modern Algebra

~1650 BCE
Rhind Papyrus — Egyptian Equations
Egyptian scribes solve linear problems of the type "a heap and its seventh makes 19" using the method of false position. No symbols — everything is written in words.
~250 CE
Diophantus — Arithmetica
Diophantus writes 13 books of algebraic problems using shorthand symbols. He studies whole-number solutions to polynomial equations (Diophantine equations). He names the unknown arithmos (number).
628 CE
Brahmagupta — Indian Algebra
The Indian mathematician Brahmagupta systematically solves linear and quadratic equations and gives the first rules for arithmetic with zero and negative numbers. His work reaches Baghdad within two centuries.
~820 CE
Al-Khwārizmī — The Founding Book
Al-Khwārizmī writes Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābala in Baghdad — the systematic method of al-jabr and al-muqābala for solving all linear and quadratic equations. This is the book that names algebra.
1202
Fibonacci — Liber Abaci
Leonardo of Pisa publishes Liber Abaci, introducing Islamic algebra and Hindu-Arabic numerals to Europe. Merchants gradually abandon Roman numerals, which cannot represent fractions cleanly.
1545
Cardano — Ars Magna
Gerolamo Cardano publishes the cubic and quartic formulas in Ars Magna — solving equations of degree 3 and 4 for the first time. This forces mathematicians to confront the square roots of negative numbers (imaginary numbers).
1591
Viète — Symbolic Algebra
François Viète introduces the practice of using letters for both known and unknown quantities — the first step toward modern symbolic algebra. He uses vowels for unknowns and consonants for known values.
1637
Descartes — Modern Notation
René Descartes in La Géométrie standardises using \(x\), \(y\), \(z\) for unknowns and \(a\), \(b\), \(c\) for known quantities. He also introduces the superscript for exponents: \(x^2\) instead of xx. This is the notation used today.
19th Century
Abstract Algebra
Galois, Cauchy, and later Cayley transform algebra from a science of equations into a study of abstract structures — groups, rings, and fields. The question shifts from "solve this equation" to "what is the structure of all equations of this type?"

How It Spread

Al-Khwārizmī's work reached Europe through a remarkable chain of translation and transmission — a story that spans three continents and five centuries.

9th–10th Century
Baghdad → Spain
Islamic scholars carry al-Khwārizmī's work westward into al-Andalus (Islamic Spain). Toledo and Córdoba become centres of learning where Islamic, Jewish, and Christian scholars work side by side.
12th Century
Translation Movement
The great translation movement in Toledo converts Arabic texts into Latin. Robert of Chester translates al-Khwārizmī's algebra book into Latin around 1145, making it accessible to European scholars for the first time.
13th–15th Century
Universities Adopt Algebra
European universities — Bologna, Oxford, Paris — begin teaching algebra based on al-Khwārizmī's methods. Italian merchants become the most advanced practical algebraists, devising shorthand for commercial calculations.
16th–17th Century
Symbolic Revolution
Viète, Descartes, and others transform rhetorical algebra (written in words) into symbolic algebra (written in letters and signs). This makes algebra vastly more powerful — you can manipulate symbols without thinking about what they represent.

Why It Still Matters

Modern Impact

Every time you solve for an unknown — in engineering, finance, science, or programming — you are using al-Khwārizmī's methods. But the impact goes far deeper:

  • Computer algebra systems (Wolfram Alpha, SymPy, MATLAB's Symbolic Toolbox) are direct descendants: they apply systematic reduction procedures to symbolic expressions.
  • Cryptography relies on abstract algebra — groups, rings, and fields. RSA encryption, which secures every HTTPS connection, is modular arithmetic applied to large numbers.
  • Coding theory uses polynomial algebra to detect and correct errors in data transmissions.
  • Machine learning expresses almost everything as linear algebra: matrices, vector spaces, eigenvectors — all extensions of al-Khwārizmī's "systematic solving."

In His Own Words

I have established, in my work on the restoration and balancing of equations, a concise method for solving all that is easiest and most useful in arithmetic — such as men constantly require in cases of inheritance, legacies, partition, lawsuits, and trade.

— Al-Khwārizmī, preface to Kitāb al-mukhtaṣar fī ḥisāb al-jabr wal-muqābala, ~820 CE

Key Takeaways

  • ✦

    The word algebra comes from al-jabr — "restoration" — one of the two operations in al-Khwārizmī's systematic method for solving equations.

  • ✦

    The word algorithm is a Latinisation of al-Khwārizmī's own name, showing how completely his work defined arithmetic procedure for medieval Europe.

  • ✦

    Al-Khwārizmī's original goal was practical — dividing inheritances and measuring land — not abstract. The most powerful mathematics often emerges from concrete, urgent needs.

  • ✦

    Modern symbolic notation (\(x\), \(y\), superscripts) came 800 years after al-Khwārizmī; his proofs were geometric, not symbolic. The notation changed everything.
