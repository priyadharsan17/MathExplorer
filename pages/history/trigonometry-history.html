Trigonometry — Mapping the Heavens

Astronomers who needed to predict where Jupiter would be in three months, or calculate the distance from the Earth to the Moon, invented a new branch of mathematics: the systematic study of the relationship between angles and distances.

The Problem

What forced this invention?

The night sky is an enormous triangle problem. You can observe the angle between two stars from Earth, and you can measure how those angles change as the Earth moves around the Sun. But you cannot physically travel to a star and measure the distance with a ruler. The only tool you have is angles — and you need to convert those angles into distances.

Ancient astronomers needed to answer questions like: How far is the Moon from Earth? What is the Earth's circumference? When will a solar eclipse occur, and where will it be visible? All of these reduce to the same mathematical challenge: given some angles and some distances in a triangle, find the rest.

The Babylonians had catalogued star positions for centuries before the Greeks. The Egyptians had built monuments aligned with astronomical precision. But neither culture had the mathematical framework to systematically relate angles to lengths. The missing tool was trigonometry — a table of how chord length (and later: sine, cosine, and tangent) changes with angle.

Hipparchus of Nicaea (~150 BCE) was the first to build such a table systematically. He did not call the values "sines" — he computed chords of a circle — but the underlying idea was identical. Six centuries later, Indian mathematician Aryabhata made the conceptual leap to the half-chord (jyā), which became the modern sine.

The People

🌟
Hipparchus of Nicaea
~190 – ~120 BCE
Astronomer, Mathematician — Nicaea & Rhodes

Often called the "father of trigonometry," Hipparchus compiled the first known trigonometric table: a table of chord lengths for a circle of fixed radius, at half-degree intervals from 0° to 180°. He used this table to calculate the distance to the Moon, estimate the length of the solar year to within six minutes of the modern value, and discover the precession of the equinoxes. None of his works survive directly — we know of them through Ptolemy's Almagest.

🔭
Claudius Ptolemy
~100 – ~170 CE
Astronomer, Mathematician — Alexandria, Egypt

Ptolemy's Almagest (the "great treatise") is the most comprehensive surviving work of ancient astronomy. It contains a detailed chord table accurate to 0.5° intervals — far more precise than Hipparchus's. Ptolemy also proved what is now called Ptolemy's theorem relating the diagonals and sides of a cyclic quadrilateral, which he used to derive his chord values. The Almagest remained the authoritative astronomical text in Europe and the Islamic world for 1,400 years.

🇮🇳
Aryabhata
476 – 550 CE
Mathematician, Astronomer — Kusumapura (modern Patna), India

Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya (499 CE) introduced the half-chord function — what we now call the sine — replacing Hipparchus's chord of the full arc. This conceptual simplification (using half the chord of twice the angle) makes all trigonometric identities cleaner. Aryabhata compiled a sine table for 24 values from 0° to 90° at 3.75° intervals, accurate to four decimal places. He also correctly calculated π ≈ 3.1416 and asserted the Earth rotates on its axis.

🌙
Al-Battānī (Albategnius)
858 – 929 CE
Astronomer, Mathematician — Raqqa (modern Syria)

Al-Battānī improved on Ptolemy's measurements with new astronomical observations and introduced the tangent and cotangent functions. He expressed trigonometric relationships as ratios (the modern approach) rather than as chord lengths. His work was translated into Latin in the 12th century and directly influenced Copernicus, Tycho Brahe, and Galileo. Copernicus cited Al-Battānī in De revolutionibus.

The Breakthrough — From Chord to Sine

The most important conceptual step in the history of trigonometry was Aryabhata's shift from Hipparchus's chord to the modern sine. It seems like a small change — but it turned a table of measurements into an elegant mathematical function.

Hipparchus's Chord (Greek)
\[ \text{crd}(\theta) = 2R \sin\!\left(\frac{\theta}{2}\right) \]
The chord of angle θ is the length of the straight line across a circle of radius R that subtends angle θ at the centre. Hipparchus used R = 3,438 (chosen so that one arc-minute of chord ≈ 1 unit).
Aryabhata's Half-Chord (Sine)
\[ \sin(\theta) = \frac{\text{crd}(2\theta)}{2R} \]
Aryabhata used the half-chord of twice the angle. For a unit circle (R = 1), this becomes exactly the modern sine function. The Sanskrit word jyā (half-chord) was transliterated by Arabic scholars as jiba, then mis-read as jaib (bay/bosom), giving the Latin sinus — and the English word sine.
The Fundamental Identities
\[ \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1 \]
The Pythagorean identity — the most important identity in trigonometry. It follows directly from the definition of sine and cosine on the unit circle, and was implicit in Aryabhata's half-chord formulation.
Al-Battānī's Tangent
\[ \tan\theta = \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta} \]
Al-Battānī introduced the tangent as the ratio of sine to cosine — and computed the first known tangent table. This ratio form replaced earlier shadow-length definitions and made the relationship between trigonometric functions explicit.
Where does the word "sine" come from? Aryabhata called the half-chord jyā in Sanskrit. Arabic translators heard this as jiba (جيب), which in Arabic also means bay or bosom. When European scholars translated from Arabic to Latin, they translated the meaning rather than the sound — choosing sinus (Latin: bay, curve). This is the direct origin of the English word sine.

Timeline — Angles, Arcs, and Stars

~1,800 BCE
Babylonian Angle Measurements
Babylonian astronomers divide the circle into 360 degrees (reflecting their base-60 number system and the approximate number of days in a year). They catalogue star positions using angle measurements. No trigonometric functions yet — only raw observations.
~240 BCE
Eratosthenes Measures the Earth
Using shadow angles at two locations (Alexandria and Syene) and the geometry of parallel rays of sunlight, Eratosthenes calculates the Earth's circumference as approximately 39,375 km — within 2% of the correct value. A triumph of applied angle geometry before formal trig tables existed.
~150 BCE
Hipparchus — First Trigonometric Table
Hipparchus compiles the first systematic table of chord values. He uses it to calculate the distance to the Moon (estimated at 59–67 Earth radii; modern value ≈ 60.3 ER) and discovers the precession of the equinoxes — the 26,000-year wobble of Earth's rotational axis.
~100 – 170 CE
Ptolemy — Almagest
Ptolemy's Almagest contains a chord table for 360 values at 0.5° intervals, accurate to 7 significant figures. Ptolemy uses the table to construct his geocentric model of planetary motion — enormously complex, but accurate enough for navigation for 1,400 years.
499 CE
Aryabhata — Sine Table and the Half-Chord
Aryabhata's Aryabhatiya introduces the sine (as jyā, half-chord) and provides a sine table for 24 angles. He also gives a recursive formula for computing sine differences — the first algorithm for building a trig table from scratch. His value of π ≈ 3.14159 is accurate to four decimal places.
~628 CE
Brahmagupta — Interpolation
Brahmagupta provides an interpolation formula for computing sine values between the tabulated angles. This is an early form of numerical analysis — approximating a continuous function from discrete data points.
858 – 929 CE
Al-Battānī — Tangent, Cotangent, New Observations
Al-Battānī introduces the tangent and cotangent, rewrites Ptolemy's results using the sine rather than the chord, and makes new precise measurements of the solar year and the obliquity of the ecliptic (Earth's axial tilt). His star catalogue improves on Ptolemy's by 700 years of new observations.
1464
Regiomontanus — First European Trig Textbook
Johannes Müller (Regiomontanus) writes De triangulis omnimodis — the first European treatise devoted entirely to trigonometry, treating it as an independent branch of mathematics rather than a tool of astronomy. The six trigonometric functions appear together for the first time.
1614
Napier's Logarithms
John Napier invents logarithms, transforming trigonometric computation. The product-to-sum identity \(\sin A \cdot \sin B = \frac{1}{2}[\cos(A-B) - \cos(A+B)]\) allowed astronomers to reduce multiplication to addition using log tables — the dominant calculation method for the next 350 years.
18th Century
Euler — Functions on the Unit Circle
Leonhard Euler reformulates trigonometry in terms of the unit circle and introduces the notation sin(x), cos(x), tan(x) that we use today. His formula \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\) unifies trigonometry with exponential functions and complex numbers — one of the deepest connections in all of mathematics.

How It Spread

Trigonometry's transmission from Greece to India to the Islamic world to Europe is one of the great stories of intellectual diffusion in history.

Greece → India
~150 BCE → ~499 CE
Greek astronomical texts (including Hipparchus's) were translated into Sanskrit. Indian mathematicians absorbed the chord-based approach and transformed it, replacing chords with half-chords (sines) and developing their own, more precise tables.
India → Islamic World
~700–900 CE
The Abbasid translation movement brought Aryabhata's and Brahmagupta's works to Baghdad. The word jyā became jiba (جيب) in Arabic. Al-Battānī and others extended the Indian approach, adding tangent and cotangent and improving tables.
Islamic World → Europe
~1000–1400 CE
Arabic trig texts reached Europe through two main routes: the translation schools of Toledo (Spain) and Sicily. The word jiba was mistranslated as sinus (Latin for bay), giving us the modern word sine. Al-Battānī's tables were used by Copernicus and Tycho Brahe.

Why It Still Matters

Modern Impact

Trigonometry is one of the most practically important branches of mathematics, embedded in technology we use every day:

  • Signal processing and audio — every sound wave is a sum of sine waves (Fourier series). MP3 compression, audio equalisation, and noise cancellation all depend on trigonometric decomposition of signals.
  • GPS and satellite navigation — satellite triangulation uses the law of cosines and spherical trigonometry. Your phone's GPS position is a trigonometric calculation performed thousands of times per second.
  • Computer graphics and games — 3D rotation, camera projection, and animation all use rotation matrices built from sine and cosine values.
  • Electrical engineering — alternating current (AC) is described by sinusoidal functions. Power grids, transformers, and wireless communications are designed using trigonometric analysis.
  • Quantum mechanics — wave functions in quantum mechanics are expressed as complex exponentials \(e^{i\theta}\). Euler's formula connects trig to quantum physics via \(e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta\).

In His Words

The circle of the sky, measured by degrees, contains three hundred and sixty. The sines of each degree, calculated by me with great effort, I here set forth.

— Aryabhata, Aryabhatiya, 499 CE (paraphrased translation)
