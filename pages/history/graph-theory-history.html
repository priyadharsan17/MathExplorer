1736 CE

Graph Theory & the Birth of Discrete Math

A leisurely stroll around a Prussian city, an impossible puzzle about seven bridges, and a single letter from Leonhard Euler to the St Petersburg Academy — that is how one of the most powerful branches of modern mathematics was born on a Sunday afternoon in 1736.

The Problem

What forced this invention?

The city of Königsberg (now Kaliningrad, Russia) was built on two islands in the Pregel River, connected to the mainland and to each other by seven bridges. The citizens of Königsberg had a popular puzzle: can you walk through the city crossing each of the seven bridges exactly once and return to your starting point?

People had tried every route they could think of and always failed — they either skipped a bridge, crossed one twice, or couldn't get back to the start. But could you be certain it was truly impossible? Maybe the right route just hadn't been found yet. No one had a mathematical argument for impossibility — only the experience of repeated failure.

The problem reached Leonhard Euler in St Petersburg in 1735. Euler wrote that "this type of solution bears little relationship to mathematics, and I do not understand why you expect a mathematician to produce it, rather than anyone else, for the solution is based on reason alone." But then he solved it — and in doing so invented an entirely new branch of mathematics.

Euler's key insight was abstraction: ignore the actual geography of Königsberg — the distances, the streets, the islands. Replace each land mass with a point (a vertex) and each bridge with a line connecting two points (an edge). The problem becomes purely about the structure of connections, not the physical layout. This act of abstraction created graph theory.

The Person

🎯
Leonhard Euler
1707 – 1783
Mathematician — Basel / St Petersburg / Berlin

The most prolific mathematician in history: his collected works fill over 80 volumes. Euler introduced or standardised most of the notation we use today: \(f(x)\) for functions, \(e\) for the base of natural logarithms, \(i\) for \(\sqrt{-1}\), \(\pi\) for the circle ratio, \(\Sigma\) for summation. He solved the Königsberg Bridge problem in 1736, published topology's Euler characteristic formula, proved Fermat's Little Theorem, and made fundamental contributions to number theory, calculus, mechanics, and optics. He lost sight in his right eye at 28, then went completely blind at 59 — and his mathematical output actually increased. He produced nearly half of all his work blind, calculating in his head.

🎨
Francis Guthrie
1831 – 1899
Mathematician / Botanist — London, England

While colouring a map of England in 1852, Guthrie noticed he needed only four colours to ensure no two adjacent regions shared a colour. He asked his brother Frederick whether any map could be coloured with four colours — and whether this had been proved. This question, passed to Augustus De Morgan and eventually to the mathematics community, became the Four Colour Theorem — one of the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics for 124 years.

🔗
Paul Erdős
1913 – 1996
Mathematician — Stateless (lived from colleague to colleague)

Erdős was the 20th century's most collaborative mathematician — he co-authored papers with 511 different people, giving rise to the concept of the "Erdős number" (how many steps of co-authorship connect you to him). He made major contributions to graph theory, combinatorics, number theory, and probability. Famously homeless by choice, he carried all his possessions in two half-empty suitcases and showed up at colleagues' homes saying "my brain is open."

The Breakthrough — Euler's Proof

Euler's proof that the Königsberg bridge walk is impossible is a masterpiece of the abstraction-first approach. He reduced a real-world puzzle to a pure structural property — parity of vertex degrees — and proved a general theorem that applies to any network.

Euler's Key Observation

In any Eulerian circuit (a walk crossing every edge exactly once and returning to the start), every vertex must be entered and exited the same number of times. This means every vertex must have an even number of edges — an even degree.

In the Königsberg graph, the four land masses have edge counts of 3, 3, 5, and 3 — all odd. Since every vertex has an odd number of edges, an Eulerian circuit is impossible. It's not that no one has found the right route — it is logically, provably impossible for any such route to exist.

Eulerian Circuit Condition
\[ \deg(v) \text{ is even for all vertices } v \]
A connected graph has an Eulerian circuit (visits every edge exactly once, returns to start) if and only if every vertex has even degree. The Königsberg graph fails this: three vertices have degree 3 and one has degree 5.
Eulerian Path Condition
\[ \text{exactly 0 or 2 vertices have odd degree} \]
If exactly two vertices have odd degree, an Eulerian path (not a circuit) exists, starting at one odd-degree vertex and ending at the other. For a circuit (returning to start), all degrees must be even.
Euler Characteristic
\[ V - E + F = 2 \]
For any connected planar graph: vertices minus edges plus faces equals 2. For a cube: 8 − 12 + 6 = 2. For a tetrahedron: 4 − 6 + 4 = 2. This topological invariant, also discovered by Euler, links graph theory to the geometry of surfaces.
What is a graph? A graph is an abstract structure consisting of vertices (points) and edges (connections between pairs of vertices). The key feature: only the existence of connections matters, not their length, position, or direction. Euler's Königsberg paper was the first to use this abstraction — and implicitly defined the object that would be studied for the next three centuries.

Timeline — From Seven Bridges to the Internet

1736
Euler — Königsberg Bridge Problem
Euler publishes "Solutio problematis ad geometriam situs pertinentis" (Solution of a problem relating to the geometry of position) — the founding paper of graph theory and topology. He proves the bridge walk is impossible using the parity argument and introduces the graph abstraction.
1752
Euler Characteristic: V − E + F = 2
Euler discovers the formula \(V - E + F = 2\) for connected planar graphs. This connects graph theory to topology — the formula holds regardless of how the graph is drawn, as long as no edges cross. It is the first topological invariant.
1852
Four Colour Conjecture Posed
Francis Guthrie, while colouring a map of England, conjectures that four colours suffice to colour any planar map so that no two adjacent regions share a colour. He passes the question to De Morgan, who cannot prove it. The conjecture remains unsolved for 124 years.
1859
Hamilton — Icosian Game and Hamiltonian Paths
William Rowan Hamilton invents the Icosian Game: find a cycle visiting all 20 vertices of a dodecahedron exactly once. This introduces the Hamiltonian path/cycle problem — still unsolved in general (it is NP-complete). The contrast with Euler's easily-solved problem highlights how sensitive graph properties are to seemingly small changes.
1936
König — First Graph Theory Textbook
Dénes König publishes Theorie der endlichen und unendlichen Graphen — the first book devoted to graph theory. It systematises two centuries of results and launches graph theory as a recognised mathematical discipline.
1959
Erdős–Rényi — Random Graph Theory
Erdős and Rényi introduce the random graph \(G(n,p)\) — a graph on n vertices where each edge exists independently with probability p. They discover phase transitions: as p increases past a threshold of \(1/n\), a giant connected component suddenly appears. This models the emergence of connectivity in networks.
1976
Four Colour Theorem Proved — by Computer
Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken prove the Four Colour Theorem — but their proof requires checking 1,936 special cases by computer. The first major theorem proved with substantial computer assistance, sparking philosophical debate about whether computer-verified proofs count as mathematical understanding.
1998
Google PageRank — Graph Theory Meets the Web
Larry Page and Sergey Brin develop PageRank — an algorithm that ranks web pages by treating the World Wide Web as a directed graph and computing the stationary distribution of a random walk. The mathematical foundation is the Perron-Frobenius theorem for non-negative matrices. Graph theory now drives the world's most important search engine.
Today
Graphs Are Everywhere
Social networks, road networks, the internet, the power grid, protein interaction networks, gene regulatory networks, knowledge graphs, and neural networks are all modelled as graphs. Graph algorithms — shortest path, minimum spanning tree, maximum flow, graph colouring — are the computational backbone of logistics, communications, biology, and AI.

Key Graph Theory Concepts

The vocabulary Euler introduced in 1736 has grown into a rich discipline. Here are the ideas that power modern applications:

Shortest Path
Dijkstra's Algorithm (1956)
Find the minimum-weight path between two vertices. Used in GPS navigation, network routing (OSPF protocol), and logistics optimisation. Powers the route calculation in every mapping app.
Spanning Tree
Kruskal, Prim (1956–1957)
Connect all vertices with the minimum total edge weight and no cycles. Used to lay out cable networks, design communication backbones, and build efficient electrical grids.
Graph Colouring
Four Colour Problem (1852)
Assign colours to vertices so no two adjacent vertices share a colour. Used in scheduling (timetabling, register allocation in compilers), frequency assignment in wireless networks, and Sudoku solving.
Network Flow
Ford–Fulkerson (1956)
Find the maximum flow through a network with capacity constraints. Models traffic, supply chains, internet bandwidth, and was used in Operations Research during WWII. The max-flow min-cut theorem is a central result.

Why It Still Matters

Modern Impact

Graph theory is the mathematics of connections — and connections are what the modern world runs on:

  • The internet and the web — the physical internet is a graph of routers and cables. Routing protocols (BGP, OSPF) are shortest-path graph algorithms. Google's PageRank, Facebook's friend suggestions, and LinkedIn's "people you may know" are all graph computations.
  • Social network analysis — modelling the spread of information, diseases, or behaviour through a population requires graph theory. Contact tracing during COVID-19 was graph-theoretic epidemiology.
  • Logistics and transport — the Travelling Salesman Problem (find the shortest route visiting all cities) is a Hamiltonian cycle problem. Despite being NP-hard, approximation algorithms power UPS, FedEx, and airline route optimisation.
  • Chip design — VLSI (very-large-scale integration) layout requires placing millions of transistors on a chip with connections that don't cross. Graph planarity testing and colouring algorithms make this possible.
  • AI and knowledge graphs — knowledge graphs (like Google's Knowledge Graph) represent real-world entities and their relationships as graphs. Graph neural networks (GNNs) learn from graph-structured data — used in drug discovery, chemistry, and recommender systems.

In His Words

The branch of geometry that deals with magnitudes has been zealously studied throughout the past, but there is another branch that has been almost unknown up to now; Leibniz spoke of it first, calling it the geometry of position. This branch is concerned with relations dependent on position alone, and investigates the properties of position; it does not take magnitudes into consideration.

— Leonhard Euler, opening of the Königsberg bridge paper, 1736 — describing what we now call graph theory and topology

Key Takeaways

  • ✦

    Graph theory was born in 1736 when Euler proved that the Königsberg bridge walk is impossible. His proof introduced the graph abstraction — vertices and edges — and the concept of vertex degree.

  • ✦

    A graph has an Eulerian circuit (traversing every edge once and returning to start) if and only if every vertex has even degree. This is Euler's theorem.

  • ✦

    The Euler characteristic \(V - E + F = 2\) is a topological invariant for connected planar graphs — unchanged by deformation. The first result connecting graphs to topology.

  • ✦

    The Four Colour Theorem (any planar map needs at most 4 colours) was conjectured in 1852 and proved in 1976 — the first major theorem proved using a computer.

  • ✦

    Google's PageRank, GPS routing, social networks, chip design, and drug discovery all depend on graph algorithms that trace directly to Euler's 1736 paper about a bridge walk.
