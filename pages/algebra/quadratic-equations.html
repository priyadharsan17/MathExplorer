Quadratic Equations

Quadratic equations introduce curves — the parabola. They model everything from projectile motion to the shape of satellite dishes, and are solved by factoring, completing the square, or the quadratic formula.

What is a Quadratic Equation?

A quadratic equation has the standard form \( ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \), where \( a \ne 0 \). The graph of \( y = ax^2 + bx + c \) is always a parabola — a symmetric U-shaped curve.

The solutions (also called roots or zeros) are the x-values where the parabola crosses the x-axis. A quadratic can have two real roots, one repeated root, or no real roots — determined entirely by the discriminant \( \Delta = b^2 - 4ac \).

The coefficient \(a\) controls the direction (up when \(a > 0\), down when \(a < 0\)) and the width of the parabola. The vertex — the highest or lowest point — is located at \( x = -\dfrac{b}{2a} \).

Key Formulas

Standard Form
\[ ax^2 + bx + c = 0 \quad (a \ne 0) \]
The canonical form used to apply the quadratic formula
Quadratic Formula
\[ x = \frac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a} \]
Works for every quadratic — always reliable when factoring is hard
Discriminant
\[ \Delta = b^2 - 4ac \]
\(\Delta > 0\): two real roots  |  \(\Delta = 0\): one root  |  \(\Delta < 0\): no real roots
Vertex
\[ x_v = -\frac{b}{2a}, \quad y_v = c - \frac{b^2}{4a} \]
The turning point of the parabola — minimum if \(a>0\), maximum if \(a<0\)
Vertex Form
\[ y = a(x - h)^2 + k \]
Vertex at \((h, k)\) — easy to read off the turning point directly

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Solving by factoring
Solve: \(x^2 - 5x + 6 = 0\)
1
Find two numbers that multiply to 6 and add to −5: they are −2 and −3.
2
Factor: (x − 2)(x − 3) = 0
3
Apply zero-product property: x = 2 or x = 3
Example 2 — Using the quadratic formula
Solve: \(2x^2 - 4x - 6 = 0\)
1
Identify: \(a = 2,\; b = -4,\; c = -6\). Compute discriminant: \(\Delta = (-4)^2 - 4(2)(-6) = 16 + 48 = 64\)
2
Apply formula: \(\displaystyle x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{64}}{4} = \frac{4 \pm 8}{4}\)
3
Two solutions: x = (4 + 8)/4 = 3 and x = (4 − 8)/4 = −1
Example 3 — No real roots (negative discriminant)
Solve: \(x^2 + x + 1 = 0\)
1
Compute discriminant: \(\Delta = 1^2 - 4(1)(1) = 1 - 4 = -3\)
2
Since \(\Delta < 0\), there are no real solutions. The parabola never touches the x-axis.

Key Takeaways

◈

The discriminant \(\Delta = b^2 - 4ac\) tells you how many real roots exist — without solving the equation.

◈

When \(a > 0\) the parabola opens upward (has a minimum); when \(a < 0\) it opens downward (has a maximum).

◈

The vertex \(\left(-\dfrac{b}{2a},\; c - \dfrac{b^2}{4a}\right)\) is the axis of symmetry — the parabola is a mirror image around it.

◈

Always check for factoring first — it's faster. Use the quadratic formula as a guaranteed fallback.
