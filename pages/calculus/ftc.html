FTC

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus

The Fundamental Theorem of Calculus is the bridge connecting differentiation and integration — two operations that appear unrelated until you see that they are exact inverses of each other.

Two Parts, One Big Idea

FTC Part 1 says: if you define an accumulation function \(G(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt\), then \(G'(x) = f(x)\). In other words, differentiating an integral returns the original integrand.

FTC Part 2 (the evaluation theorem) says: if \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\), then \(\int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a)\). This turns area calculation into simple subtraction — no need for Riemann sum limits.

Together they say: integration and differentiation undo each other, exactly as multiplication and division do in arithmetic.

The Two Parts

FTC Part 1
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\left[\int_a^x f(t)\,dt\right] = f(x) \]
Differentiating the area-accumulation function recovers \(f\).
FTC Part 2
\[ \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
Where \(F\) is any antiderivative of \(f\); the \(+C\) cancels out.
Inverse Relationship
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x) \quad \text{and} \quad \int_a^b F'(x)\,dx = F(b) - F(a) \]
Both parts express that \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\) and \(\int\) are inverses.
Chain Rule Extension
\[ \frac{d}{dx}\int_a^{g(x)} f(t)\,dt = f(g(x))\cdot g'(x) \]
When the upper bound is a function of \(x\), apply chain rule.

Worked Examples

Example 1 — Using FTC Part 2
Evaluate \(\displaystyle\int_1^3 (2x)\,dx\)
1
Antiderivative: \(F(x) = x^2\)
2
\(F(3) - F(1) = 9 - 1 = 8\)
✓
\(\int_1^3 2x\,dx = 8\)
Example 2 — Applying FTC Part 1
Find \(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin(t^2)\,dt\)
1
FTC Part 1 directly: the derivative of the accumulation integral is the integrand evaluated at the upper bound.
✓
\(\dfrac{d}{dx}\displaystyle\int_0^x \sin(t^2)\,dt = \sin(x^2)\)

Key Takeaways

◈

FTC Part 1: differentiating an integral returns the integrand — \(\tfrac{d}{dx}\int_a^x f(t)\,dt = f(x)\).

◈

FTC Part 2: a definite integral equals \(F(b) - F(a)\) — the difference of antiderivative values at the endpoints.

◈

The \(+C\) always cancels in FTC Part 2, so any antiderivative works.

◈

FTC is why we compute area by finding antiderivatives rather than taking limits of sums.
